Beyond Student Numbers: The Future of Higher Education Recruitment Across South Asia, Europe and the UK

By Md Sabbir Khan · International Higher Education, Partnerships & Student Recruitment Leader
Published 7 October 2026 · Last updated 7 October 2026 · 10 min read

International student recruitment is entering a more demanding era. Universities that continue to measure success primarily by application and enrolment volumes risk missing a much bigger transformation taking place across global higher education.

For more than 12 years, my professional journey has taken me across international student recruitment, university representation, market development, institutional partnerships and, more recently, UK home and local student recruitment.

I have worked across both sides of the sector: representing institutions directly in international markets and developing recruitment and partnership strategies connecting universities, education partners and prospective students.

That experience has reinforced one conclusion for me:

The future of higher education recruitment will not be won by the institution with the largest agent network or the highest number of applications. It will be won by institutions that understand their markets, build the right partnerships, recruit the right students and remain accountable for what happens after enrolment.

South Asia Is Not One Higher Education Market

One of the biggest mistakes institutions can make is treating South Asia as a single recruitment market.

Bangladesh is not India. India is not Nepal. Pakistan is not Sri Lanka.

And even within an individual country, student behaviour can differ significantly by region, socioeconomic background, education history, subject interest and intended destination.

A recruitment strategy developed centrally in the UK and distributed across South Asia without sufficient local intelligence may generate applications, but that does not necessarily mean it will generate sustainable enrolments.

Universities need to understand much more than market size. They need to understand student motivation, affordability, academic progression, family influence, agent behaviour, competitor activity, visa risk, employability expectations and changing perceptions of study destinations. (I looked at several of these drivers for one market in Bangladesh: South Asia's second-largest market for international student recruitment.)

This is where genuine market leadership becomes important. A strong regional strategy should not simply answer:

“How many students can we recruit?”

It should answer:

“Which students should we recruit, through which channels, for which programmes, and with what likelihood of successful enrolment and progression?”

Recruitment Volume Is No Longer Enough

For years, international recruitment strategies frequently celebrated numbers: 500 agents. 1,000 agents. Thousands of applications. Large enquiry pipelines.

But those figures can be misleading.

An agent network is only valuable when the partners within it are active, knowledgeable, compliant and capable of recruiting students who are genuinely suitable for the institution.

I would rather see an institution build 100 accountable, productive and well-trained relationships than maintain 1,000 largely inactive names in a database. (I made the same argument in The Mr Khan Podcast: universities don't need more agents, they need better agents.)

The same principle applies to applications. A large volume of poorly matched applications creates additional work for admissions teams, increases compliance exposure and produces weak conversion.

The more meaningful recruitment journey is:

Enquiry → Qualified Applicant → Offer → Deposit → CAS/Confirmation → Enrolment → Retention → Completion → Outcome

If we measure only the beginning of that journey, we are measuring activity rather than success.

Compliance Is Becoming a Recruitment Strategy

The UK market in particular is demonstrating why recruitment and compliance can no longer operate as separate conversations.

Universities face significant responsibilities when sponsoring international students, and institutional risk can be affected by recruitment quality, visa outcomes, enrolment behaviour and student progression. Recent sponsor licence suspensions and recruitment pauses by some UK universities show how directly this now affects recruitment decisions.

This changes the role of recruitment professionals and education partners. The objective cannot simply be:

“Get the application.”

It must become:

“Is this student academically appropriate, financially prepared, genuinely motivated and realistically suited to this course and institution?”

This requires better counselling at the beginning of the process. It also requires universities to look more carefully at how their recruitment partners operate.

Strong partnerships should involve training, performance measurement, market intelligence, compliance awareness, transparency and accountability.

Responsible recruitment is not the enemy of growth. It is what makes long-term growth possible.

From South Asia to Europe: Recruitment Requires Different Thinking

My work has increasingly reinforced another important point: the international recruitment model cannot simply be copied from one region and applied to another.

South Asia may require extensive engagement with education agents, families and local recruitment networks.

Other markets may respond more strongly to institutional reputation, digital discovery, professional networks or direct university engagement.

European recruitment presents its own dynamics.

And inside the UK itself, there is another market that international recruitment teams sometimes overlook: home and local students. This is particularly important as institutions seek greater diversification and resilience.

International and Home Recruitment Should Learn From Each Other

My involvement in UK home and local student recruitment has given me a different perspective on the recruitment journey.

International recruitment and domestic recruitment are often managed separately, and there are good reasons for that. The audiences, funding mechanisms, admissions considerations and decision-making journeys can be very different.

But strategically, they can learn from each other.

Home recruitment can teach international teams about local community engagement, accessibility, flexible education pathways and reaching students who may not follow the traditional university journey.

International recruitment can bring expertise in market development, relationship management, conversion, multicultural communication and partnership-led growth.

The future may therefore require universities to think less in terms of isolated recruitment channels and more in terms of an integrated student-market strategy.

The Opportunity Beyond Traditional Recruitment

Another major shift is already emerging.

For decades, internationalisation was often interpreted primarily as bringing international students to a university campus. That model remains important. But it is no longer the whole opportunity.

The next phase of international higher education will increasingly involve:

  • transnational education (TNE)
  • institutional partnerships
  • articulation and progression arrangements
  • online and blended education
  • local delivery
  • research collaboration
  • professional education
  • new forms of cross-border academic cooperation.

This changes the strategic question. Instead of asking only:

“How many students can South Asia send to the UK?”

we should also ask:

“What can UK institutions build with South Asia?”

That is a fundamentally different conversation. It moves the relationship from recruitment to partnership.

Technology and AI Will Change Recruitment, But Not Replace Trust

Artificial intelligence will inevitably reshape higher education recruitment.

AI can already support enquiry management, lead qualification, communication, data analysis, applicant matching and administrative processes. Universities and education organisations that ignore these developments will become less efficient.

But I do not believe technology eliminates the need for human expertise.

International education involves major financial, academic and personal decisions. Students and their families still need judgement, context and accountability. Universities still need people who understand markets and relationships.

The strongest model will therefore not be AI versus people. It will be:

Technology + Market Intelligence + Human Expertise + Trust

Technology should make good professionals more effective, not make responsible counselling disappear. (More on this in the podcast episode AI + human expertise + trust: the future of education advising.)

Student Recruitment Cannot End at Enrolment

There is another uncomfortable question our sector needs to address.

Universities invest significant resources in attracting international students. Recruitment teams focus on applications and enrolment. Academic teams focus on progression. Student services focus on support. Careers teams focus on employability.

But the student experiences all of these as one journey.

That is why I believe international student success needs to become a shared institutional responsibility. The strategic framework should become:

Recruitment → Experience → Retention → Employability → Outcomes

A successful international strategy should ultimately be measured not simply by how many students arrived, but by how successfully those students progressed.

What I Have Learned From More Than a Decade in the Sector

My career in international education began in South Asia and developed through institution-side recruitment, market development and representation roles involving international education organisations and institutions connected to Canada, Australia and the UK. (I wrote about one of those models, in-country representation, in 2024.)

Today, working from London across partnerships and business development has given me the opportunity to look at the market from an increasingly global perspective while remaining closely connected to the realities of student recruitment.

I have worked with agent networks, institutional stakeholders, international applicants and UK-based students.

I have seen markets expand. I have seen policies change. I have seen institutions enter new regions. And I have seen recruitment strategies that looked impressive on paper fail because they lacked local understanding.

The biggest lesson has been simple:

Higher education is a relationship business supported by data, not a numbers business supported by relationships.

The distinction matters.

Five Principles for the Next Generation of Higher Education Recruitment

For institutions looking at South Asia, Europe and the UK, I believe five principles will become increasingly important:

1. Market intelligence before market expansion

Understand why students behave as they do before investing heavily in recruitment.

2. Quality of partnerships before quantity of partners

Measure performance, compliance, knowledge and student outcomes, not simply network size.

3. Student fit before application volume

A smaller pipeline of appropriate applicants can be more valuable than thousands of poorly qualified leads.

4. Diversification before dependency

No institution should become dangerously dependent on one country, channel or recruitment model.

5. Student outcomes after enrolment

Recruitment success should connect with retention, completion, employability and longer-term student outcomes.

The Next Competitive Advantage Is Trust

Higher education is becoming more competitive, more regulated and more technologically sophisticated.

But amid all this change, I believe one competitive advantage will become more valuable rather than less: trust.

Students need to trust the advice they receive. Universities need to trust their recruitment partners. Partners need to trust institutions to communicate clearly and operate consistently. And the sector needs to demonstrate that international education creates genuine academic, professional and social value.

South Asia will remain an important part of the global education landscape. Europe will continue to evolve. UK home recruitment will present its own opportunities. New markets will emerge, and technology will change how we engage with all of them.

But sustainable growth will come from institutions and professionals capable of connecting market intelligence, responsible recruitment, partnership development and student success.

That is where I believe the next chapter of higher education recruitment will be written. And that is the part of the sector I want to continue helping build.
